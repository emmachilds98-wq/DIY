Electrics: your own job

You're doing the electrics yourselves. In England, Part P of the Building Regulations makes only three kinds of work notifiable: a new circuit, a replacement consumer unit, and any addition or alteration to a circuit in a special location (round a bath or shower). Replacing an accessory like for like, and adding to an existing circuit anywhere else, isn't notifiable, though it must still be done to the wiring regulations (BS 7671). So the jobs here need no electrician and no building control: the socket swapped for a double, the two dimmers, and the two spot bars in place of the old fittings. The Cat6 and the conduits aren't mains work at all.

The safe routine, every time
  1. Isolate: switch off that circuit's breaker at the consumer unit (the lighting circuit for the dimmers and bars, the socket circuit for the socket). If the labels aren't clear, switch off the main switch.
  2. Lock off: fit the MCB lock-off and padlock, keep the key in your pocket, and tape a note on the board.
  3. Prove dead: check the tester lights on its proving unit, test between every pair of terminals (live–neutral, live–earth, neutral–earth), then check the tester on the proving unit again.
  4. Photograph the wiring before you disconnect anything, and connect the new one wire for wire: brown (or red) to L, blue (or black) to N, green-and-yellow to earth, sleeved, no copper showing, every screw tight.
  5. Test: power back on, then the socket tester in both outlets; each dimmer from low to high with no flicker.
Stop and call an electrician if…
  • the tester shows anything live after you've isolated;
  • the wiring is old (rubber or fabric sheath, or red and black with no earth), or there's no earth at a ceiling point (the spot bars need one);
  • there are more wires than you can match to your photo (two-way switching, a loop in a ceiling rose you can't follow), or scorch marks, or a loose, hot or crumbling fitting;
  • the consumer unit has rewireable fuses or no RCD;
  • a job grows into a new circuit, consumer unit work, or anything by the bath or shower.

Six techniques you'll use

  1. Fixing into brick. Detect, mark, drill with the SDS on hammer setting, clear the dust, push in a brown (7 mm) wall plug and drive a 5 × 60 mm screw. In plasterboard, find a stud, or use heavy-duty plasterboard fixings rated for the load.
  2. Cutting oak cleanly. Put masking tape over the cut line, score it with a knife, cut with the good face down (circular or track saw) or up (jigsaw). Clamp a straight edge as a guide.
  3. Routing a housing (a shallow slot a shelf sits in). Clamp a straight batten as a guide, set the bit 4 mm deep, rout, then step down to 12 mm in passes. Practise on an offcut first.
  4. A wedged through-tenon. Mark the 6 × 2.4 cm mortise on both faces of the upright and drill most of it out with a 20 mm bit from each side. Chisel it to the line. Saw the tenon on the shelf's end. Saw a kerf down its middle, glue it, tap it home, then drive a glued oak wedge into the kerf. When it's dry, saw it flush and plane it.
  5. Dowelling. Use a dowel jig to drill matching 10 mm holes 3 cm deep in both parts, glue the dowels, clamp.
  6. Oiling. Sand to 180 grit, wipe off the dust, rub on a thin coat of hardwax oil, wipe off the excess after 15 minutes, let it dry overnight, give it a second coat. Do the undersides too, so the boards don't cup.

Where beginners usually go wrong

  • Cutting the worktop to the tape instead of to a template. Make a cardboard template of the alcove first; walls are rarely square.
  • Skipping the dry-fit. Gluing a tenon that doesn't fit is the one mistake you can't undo.
  • Oil on one side only. The board cups within a week.
  • Loading the trays before the anti-tip brackets go in. A full tray weighs about 32 kg.
  • Folding the table up with something on it. Clear it first, every time, and keep both turn buttons turned when it's up.
